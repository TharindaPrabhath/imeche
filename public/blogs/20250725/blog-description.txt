Recently, IMechE Student Chapter of University of Moratuwa had the honor of hosting a valuable session for undergraduates, which was on “Initiating and Executing Research and Development Projects”. The resource person for the session was Prof. Ruwan Gopura, the Dean of the Faculty of Graduate Studies at the University of Moratuwa. With over 25 years of experience as a researcher and academic mentor, Prof. Gopura offered a rich and practical perspective on how undergraduate students can meaningfully engage in Research and Development (R&D) from the early stages of their academic journey.

The purpose of the session was to close the knowledge gap between academic theory and practical research methods, particularly for final-year engineering undergraduates working on their design/research projects. Prof. Gopura began by explaining the fundamental differences between research and projects, emphasizing that research involves the pursuit of new knowledge and insights through critical inquiry, whereas projects often follow a well-defined plan with predictable outcomes. In this regard, he defined final-year undergraduate projects as chances for students to apply engineering principles through design and contribute original knowledge.

Understanding the distinctions between pure and applied research, as well as between qualitative and quantitative research, and their corresponding goals, took up a significant portion of the session.

 He pointed out that this knowledge is essential for establishing the parameters and course of a student's project. In order to give students a structured approach to their work, he also talked about the significance of defining research goals and objectives using the SMART criteria (Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Result-oriented, and Time-bound).

After that, Professor Gopura gave a detailed explanation of how to start a research project, beginning with choosing a topic of personal interest and assembling a team of people with complementary abilities. By locating pertinent supervisors, talking about potential subjects, and carrying out in-depth literature reviews to pinpoint research gaps, he urged students to take charge of their projects. He emphasized that students should synthesize recent literature to identify unresolved problems and that novelty is a crucial component of research.

The session also covered practical topics like time management, budgetary restrictions, productive teamwork, and typical mistakes made by students when working on projects. 

Professor Gopura stressed that the project should mimic an actual engineering setting, exposing students to communication, teamwork, and problem-solving techniques that are critical in the field.

The reminder that a project's success depends not only on the finished product or prototype but also on the capacity developed by the student, knowledge, skills, and experience acquired during the process was a noteworthy highlight. In order to demonstrate how undergraduate research can have a wider impact, he also urged students to strive for the dissemination of their work through conference papers, journal publications, patents, or media appearances.

Lastly, Prof. Gopura introduced several initiatives from the Faculty of Graduate Studies to support undergraduate research, including publication opportunities, involvement in the University Research Week, and a mentoring program that connects undergraduates with postgraduate students.

This session was a valuable resource for any undergraduate student aiming to embark on their research journey with clarity, confidence, and a strong foundation for future academic or industry success.
